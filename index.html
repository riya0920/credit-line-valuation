A small valuation demo

Who should get a higher credit line?

A line increase is worth the extra balance it unlocks, minus the extra loss it risks. The customers where that difference is biggest are rarely the safest ones or the biggest spenders. This page shows why on 20,000 made-up cardholders, then lays out how I would test it for real.

Each policy ranks all 20,000 cardholders and gives increases from the top of its list down. Low risk first ranks by default probability. High balance first ranks by how much people owe today. How I would test it

The chart cheats: it knows each person’s true value. Real data only shows what happened to people who got an increase, and past increases were chosen by an older policy, so comparing past recipients to everyone else mixes the effect of the increase with the effect of who was picked. The clean fix is a randomized test.

Design

Among customers who already pass ability-to-pay and policy checks, randomly give the increase to one group and hold out a matching group. Randomize by account and keep the holdout stable for the whole readout.

Primary metric

Incremental 12-month profit per account, test minus holdout: revenue on the extra balance, minus the change in charge-offs, minus cost.

Guardrails

30+ day delinquency, charge-off rate, payment rate, and complaints. A pre-agreed stop rule if test-group delinquency runs above the holdout by more than a set margin.

Readouts

Losses take months to show, so read early signals first: usage of the new room at 60 days, then delinquency at 3 and 6 months, with the final call on 12-month losses.

Build plan

  1. Run the randomized test first. It gives unbiased training labels and the yardstick every later model is judged against. It is how I tested every messaging rule at my current job.
  2. Model the two sides separately. One model for the extra balance an increase unlocks, one for the change in default risk and loss. Value is revenue minus loss minus cost, so each piece can be checked, explained, and recalibrated on its own.
  3. Rank by value and stop where it turns negative. The stopping point comes out of the curve, and leadership can move it to match risk appetite. The stress view above shows how much that point should shrink in a downturn.
  4. Put the checks in front of the model. Ability-to-pay rules first, then a fair-lending review of who gets increases, then reason codes a customer and a model reviewer can read.
  5. Monitor by vintage. Track each month’s increases against what the model promised, with drift alarms on the inputs, a small ongoing holdout to keep measuring lift, and a kill switch back to the previous policy.

What this demo leaves out

  • Only 12 months. Card losses often peak after the first year, and a longer horizon also adds retention value. The horizon choice can change who ranks on top.
  • No capital, reserve, or funding detail. Funding is folded into a flat margin.
  • Fixed behavior. How much of the new room people use is held constant. In reality it shifts with season, the economy, and how the increase is presented.
  • Checks are described, not simulated. Ability-to-pay and fair-lending reviews appear in the plan and are absent from the numbers.
  • Made-up relationships. The links between usage, balance, and risk are my assumptions. A real version would estimate them from the randomized test.
